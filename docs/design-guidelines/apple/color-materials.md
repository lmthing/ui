# Color, depth, and Liquid Glass

## Roles before colors

Use semantic foreground and surface roles: primary text for essential information, secondary for supporting detail, accent for a primary action or selection, and separate status roles for warning, error, and success. Don't turn the accent into the destructive color just because the preset looks warm. Supply a second cue for every essential status (label, icon, position, or pattern). Use dynamic system colors on native rather than sampling their default appearance. Evaluate light, dark, and higher contrast with actual content behind each layer. A test against the average color of a photo can miss the bright patch that passes under a button.

For routine text, use **4.5:1** as the cross-platform baseline; aim for **7:1** for custom small text where feasible, consistent with Apple's Dark Mode guidance. Web WCAG AA permits 3:1 for large text and calls for 3:1 for essential non-text contrast against adjacent colors. Verify the composited foreground/background, not the token values in isolation. Check disabled controls for understandability even when exempt from a contrast threshold. Don't confuse iOS raised surfaces in dark mode with an extra border on every nested container.

## Content layer versus control layer

Introduced in the iOS 26 design, Liquid Glass is a **functional layer above content**. System bars, tool controls, and certain inset sheets can float over the page and adapt to what scrolls underneath. Lensing, moving highlights, adaptive shadow and contrast, and the touch response belong to the material's behavior; a semitransparent fill is not the same thing. Apple's WWDC26 SwiftUI update previews a further refined appearance and adjustable tint for the 27 releases, another reason not to freeze the native appearance into app tokens.

- **Content:** use ordinary opaque surfaces or standard materials for reading planes, collections and cards. Keep enough contrast for labels and controls. A system switch or slider may briefly use a glass treatment on its moving part during interaction; that doesn't make the entire form a glass surface.
- **Controls:** use system chrome where possible. Use regular Liquid Glass by default when making a custom floating control. Use clear only over genuinely rich imagery where seeing through it matters; add a tested darkening treatment if a bright backdrop defeats legibility. Avoid styling clusters of competing controls as separate floating bubbles.
- **Separation:** a scroll edge effect protects floating controls where content actually moves behind them. Use one per view; don't add blur as a section divider or stack multiple effects over the same scroll edge. Let the background go edge-to-edge without burying important image subjects or any controls.
- **Geometry:** coordinate nested corners. A capsule has a radius of half its height. For a regular inset sharing the parent's corner center, the inner radius is the outer radius minus the inset (where positive); on native prefer the system concentric shape and preview the fit. Don't force a capsule around paragraphs.
- **Fallback:** system controls can respond to Reduce Transparency, Increase Contrast, Reduce Motion, and the person's glass appearance. On web, prefer a reliable opaque surface first. A supported `backdrop-filter` is optional; don't fake lensing with layers of blur and shadow. Keep a solid high-contrast fallback for forced colors, performance constraints, and browsers that can't reproduce the material. Fallbacks keep the same structure, focus, and actions.

## For lmthing

Treat a floating treatment as a shared chrome decision for `Header`, code-owned `TabBar`, and the `PhoneScreen` sheet frame; keep `Card`, `List`, dashboards, and commerce tiles on content surfaces. Audit `Hero`, `Photo` overlays, and media or weather artwork against the rubric in varying real backdrops. Gap to investigate: one semantic chrome treatment with a scroll-under boundary and opaque fallback in the kit, verified on native and web. Keep color and translucency off the model's wire format.
